# Chapter 29 — How to Invent Shaders

This is the book's philosophical core. Everything so far has been a catalog of objects. This chapter teaches the *method* for combining them into new shaders. It is the answer to the central question: **how do you go from "I want this visual" to "what mathematical system generates it" to "a shader"?**

## 29.1 The generative recipe

The most general construction is:

$$
\text{Primitive} + \text{Transformation} + \text{Repetition} + \text{Deformation} + \text{Iteration} + \text{Lighting} + \text{Color} + \text{Time}
= \text{New Shader}.
$$

Each "+" is a *mathematical operator applied to the field*, not a script step. The art is choosing which operators to apply and in what order.

## 29.2 The questions to ask of a visual

Given a desired image, ask these in order (this is the "reverse-engineer a visual" process):

1. **What symmetry does this image have?** Rotational, mirror, translational, reflectional, scaling (self-similar)? Pick the coordinate system adapted to that symmetry (Chapter 04, 11).

2. **What coordinate system makes it simple?** A ring → polar. A tunnel/corridor → axial repeat. A symmetric crystal → folded space. A radial swirl → polar + phase. This choice *collapses* the description to a few terms.

3. **What is repeating?** Find the periodic structure (rings, cells, arms, tiles). Apply `mod`, `frac`, radial wrap, or mirror fold.

4. **What is transforming?** Which coordinate/frame rotates, twists, bends, scales? Apply the corresponding domain transform (Chapter 10).

5. **What is being iterated?** Is there self-similar, recursive, or escape-time structure? Then it's a KIFS, a fractal, or a DE (Chapters 19, 20).

6. **What is the underlying field?** Identify the base SDF (a sphere, box, torus, cylinder) that the symmetries and transforms modify.

7. **Where does the complexity actually come from?** Usually *not* from many primitives, but from a few primitives combined with repetition and iteration. Ask whether the complexity can be *generated* (by repetition/folding/iteration) instead of *modeled* (by placing many objects).

## 29.3 The "complexity from a small system" principle

The reason a few lines of GLSL can make a rich image is that the complexity is *procedural*: it is generated by a small set of operations applied over a domain. The trick is to recognize that:

$$
\text{small mathematical system}\ \times\ \text{large domain}\ =\ \text{large visual complexity}.
$$

Repetition (Chapter 11) and iteration (Chapter 19) are the two engines of this. A single primitive, repeated, becomes a whole world; a single map, iterated, becomes a fractal. **Design the *system*, not the *object*.**

## 29.4 A worked invention: "a rotating crystalline tunnel"

**Step 1 — Visual target.** A tunnel with faceted crystal walls, rotating slowly.

**Step 2 — Reduce to geometry.**
```
tunnel (cylinder)
+ radial repetition (fold the angle into facets)
+ axial repetition (a corridor)
+ rotation (isometry)
+ surface lighting.
```

**Step 3 — Equations.**

Base: infinite cylinder $d_{\text{cyl}}=\lVert\mathbf p_{xz}\rVert-r$.

Facets: fold the angle $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(p_z,p_x)$ into an $n$-fold sector and rebuild a symmetric cross-section.

Repetition: $p_y'=\operatorname{mod}(p_y+a/2,a)-a/2$.

Rotation: $\mathbf p'=R(\omega t)\mathbf p$ (exact isometry).

**Step 4 — Analyze.** The cylinder and repetition are exact SDFs; the radial fold is an isometry (exact); the rotation is an exact isometry. The facet fold is exact if the cross-section is symmetric. Total field is (near-)exact → safe, fast, clean edges.

**Step 5 — Ray marcher.** Standard sphere-trace.

**Step 6 — Normals.** Central differences.

**Step 7 — Lighting.** Lambert + GGX + Fresnel + soft shadow.

**Step 8 — Material.** A crystal: internal glow, speckle, color from the facet angle.

**Step 9 — Animation.** Rotate the tunnel frame (isometry, coherent).
**Step 10 — Optimize.** Early-exit; use a cheap facet fold.
**Step 11 — Compress.** Recognize the "cylinder + repeat + fold" is just a few lines.
**Step 12 — Final GLSL.**

```glsl
float map(vec3 p){
    p = rotY(p, iTime*0.3);        // rotate (isometry)
    p.y = mod(p.y+2.,4.)-2.;        // axial repeat
    // radial fold into an 8-fold cross section
    float a = atan(p.z,p.x);
    a = mod(a+0.3927, 0.7854)-0.3927;  // 8 arms
    p.xz = vec2(cos(a),sin(a))*length(p.xz);
    return length(p.xz)-1.0;        // tunnel wall
}
```

**Post-mortem.** The whole "rotating crystalline tunnel" reduces to: rotate space, repeat along the axis, fold the angle into 8 sectors, measure a cylinder. The complexity (8-sided crystalline corridor, rotating) comes entirely from *repetition and symmetry*, not from modeling. That is the generative principle in action.

## 29.5 Combinatorial thinking

The power of the method is that the operators *compose*. Consider the "natural family" from one equation:

$$
d=\lVert\mathbf p\rVert-r \quad\text{(sphere)}.
$$

Then ask:

- What if $r$ varies? → a deformed blob (Chapter 16).
- What if $\mathbf p$ is transformed? → an ellipsoid / a rotated shape (Chapter 10).
- What if the sphere is repeated? → a lattice / a tunnel (Chapter 11).
- What if multiple fields are combined? → a boolean/sculpture (Chapter 9).
- What if the coordinates are warped? → a swirl / a flow (Chapter 16).
- What if the result is iterated? → a fractal/KIFS (Chapter 19).

Each "what if" is one operator. The **family of shaders** discoverable from one sphere is enormous. The method is to *systematically* explore these variations.

## 29.6 The master checklist for inventing

When designing a shader, run through the "what is" questions and then the "what if" questions, and you'll have a map of the design space:

```
Primitive (what's the base solid?)
Transformation (how is space deformed?)
Repetition (what repeats?)
Deformation (how is detail added?)
Iteration (is there self-similarity?)
Lighting (how is it lit?)
Color (how is it colored?)
Time (how does it animate?)
```

## 29.7 The philosophy

> A raymarched shader is a mathematical model evaluated millions of times by a GPU.

The implication: you *invent* the shader by inventing the **model**, not by editing code. The training is to reason from visual → geometry → coordinates → field → transforms → intersection → normal → light → color → time, and to know at each step which operators are available and which preserve the distance property.

The final skill is **generative**: to look at a visual and *ask the questions* until the mathematical system that generates it emerges. Everything else is detail.
